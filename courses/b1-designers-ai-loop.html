The designer's AI loop

A generator can produce forty screens before you finish your coffee. None of that is design. Design is the decision about which one is right and why - and that decision got more valuable the moment generation got cheap. Tonight you get the loop this whole track runs on: brief, generate, critique, direct, ship - and the uncomfortable fact that AI is fastest exactly in the middle and weakest at both ends. Then you meet the lab: a real Cadence screen you can score, live, on real measurements. By b10 you will have taken one Cadence feature from brief to a shipped, spec'd, system-conformant screen set.

Part 1 · the spine of the track

Brief, generate, critique, direct, ship 7 min live

Every AI design task runs the same five steps, and they are not equally hard. Brief: say what you want, in the language of constraints rather than adjectives. Generate: let the machine produce more options than you would have had patience for. Critique: judge what came back against a standard you can state out loud. Direct: change the brief, not the pixels - a bad output is usually a vague constraint. Ship: make it real, in a system, with rights you can defend. AI is extraordinary at step two. It is thin at step one and step three, and those are the two steps that decide whether the work is any good.

LiveThe four levers of this track4 min▶

The loop is how you work. The four levers are what a design practice needs so AI output survives contact with a real product - and they are the four ways it fails:

  • Direction - without a real brief, the model gives you the statistical average of the internet. Beautifully rendered, completely generic. (b2, b3)
  • Structure - without a grid, a scale and a component set, every generated screen is a one-off that cannot be shipped or maintained. (b4, b6)
  • Critique - without a stated standard, "I don't like it" is where the review ends and the rework begins. (b5, and tonight's lab)
  • Rights - without provenance, licensing and brand discipline, your best asset is the one legal makes you delete. (b7, and leader session a4)

Tonight's lab has a fifth toggle: Add colour. It is not a lever. Turn it on last and watch what happens to a perfect score - we will spend part of b5 on why that is the most common instinct and the most expensive one.

Self-studyWhy "make it pop" is not direction3 min read▶

A brief made of adjectives - clean, modern, premium, bold - cannot be wrong, which is exactly the problem. A brief made of constraints can be checked:

Adjective brief

"Design a clean, modern summary screen for our AI notes app. Make it feel premium and trustworthy."

Constraint brief

"Summary screen for Cadence. One primary action: Share summary. Everything else is secondary. Type on a 1.25 scale from a 16px body; spacing in multiples of 8; one accent hue (plum #6B2D5C) and neutrals only; all text must pass WCAG AA against its background; the AI-generated label must be visible but must not compete with the meeting title."

The second brief is longer, less poetic, and produces work you can accept or reject on evidence. b2 turns this into a repeatable structure; every constraint in it is something tonight's lab can measure.

Part 2 · the part of taste that is measurable

Half of taste is arithmetic 6 min live

Designers describe their judgement as intuition, and some of it genuinely is - composition, wit, restraint, whether an idea has any tension in it. But a surprising share of what we call taste is measurable, and therefore teachable to a machine and checkable by anyone: does text pass contrast, do sizes come from one scale, does spacing sit on a rhythm, is there exactly one loudest thing, how many hue families are fighting on screen. AI output usually fails on this measurable half - which is good news, because you can catch it without arguing about taste at all.

LiveWhy generated design regresses to the average4 min▶

A generator produces the most likely next pixel, the most likely layout, the most likely hero-with-gradient. Likely is the opposite of distinctive. Practically, this shows up as a recognisable signature - centred everything, one giant gradient, default-feeling type, decorative iconography that means nothing, and a colour palette that has three accents because no one decided.

  • It raises your floor. A blank page becomes forty starting points. That is real value, especially outside your own style.
  • It lowers your ceiling if you let it. The average of everything is by construction not a point of view.
  • It converges. If you and your three competitors brief the same tool the same lazy way, you will all ship the same screen.

The defence is not to refuse the tool. It is to arrive with direction the tool does not have, and a standard it cannot fake. Both are learnable, and both are what the rest of this track drills.

Self-studyWhere the measurable numbers come from3 min read▶

Two of the five checks in tonight's lab are formal standards, and three are conventions. Knowing which is which keeps you honest in a review:

  • Contrast is a standard. WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.4.3 requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, where large means 18pt (about 24px), or 14pt bold (about 18.5px by W3C's own 1pt = 1.333px conversion; tools commonly implement 18.66px). The lab computes real ratios with the WCAG relative-luminance formula.
  • Non-text contrast is a standard. SC 1.4.11 asks for 3:1 on the visual boundaries of controls and meaningful graphics - the reason a pale grey button border is a bug, not a style.
  • The type scale is a convention. Nothing requires a 1.25 ratio. What matters is that ONE ratio generates all your sizes, so the set stays small and related.
  • The 8px grid is a convention. A widely shared one, not a spec - platform guidance is stated in points and density-independent units. The value is divisibility and consistency, not the number 8 itself.
  • "One loudest thing" is a convention with strong perceptual grounding: attention goes to the largest, heaviest, highest-contrast element. Two of them means neither wins.

b5 turns these into a review script you can hand a stakeholder. b6 turns them into tokens a machine can enforce.

Real count of hue families. Start with every lever off, read the failure rows, then bring them on one at a time and watch which row moves.

Levers all off: 17/100. Read the five rows out loud. "It needs polish" is now five specific, checkable failures.

Turn on Hierarchy only. The title steps up, the shouty banner and button stand down. Watch the "3 elements shouting" row.

Add Type scale, then Spacing. Notice you are changing arithmetic, not taste - and the screen is already better.

Add Contrast. One row stays stubborn: the orange bullet mark. The third hue family was also a contrast bug.

Add One accent. Everything resolves. Then press Add colour and watch a finished screen lose points.

How to read every lab in this track The starting draft is scripted - a composite of what unbriefed generators hand you. Everything after that is measured live in your browser: contrast from the WCAG relative-luminance formula, spacing and sizes from getComputedStyle, hue families by converting every rendered colour to HSL. Demo 2 of 2

The climb - your benchmark for the whole track ★ 10 min · explore

The same screen, scored at every rung of the ladder, computed in front of you: 17 → 32 → 46 → 66 → 86 → 100. Each rung is one lever, and each lever is a session. Then the last row: with the screen at 100, turning on Add colour costs 19 points - it introduces a fourth hue family, breaks two spacing values, and puts yellow text on a yellow highlight. The most common instinct in a design review, priced.

17 - no direction. Every check fails except part of hierarchy, and that only by accident.

32 - hierarchy (this session's habit). One loudest thing, a 2.07x step from body to title.

46 → 66 - type scale and spacing (b4, b6). Six arbitrary sizes become four related ones; every gap lands on the grid.

86 - contrast (b5). 16 of 19 text elements pass AA; the survivors tell you where the extra hues are hiding.

100 - one accent (b6, b7). Measurably clean. Now go and put an idea in it, because 100 is not the same as good.

Real world

This is the fastest review you will ever run on a stakeholder's "just make it pop". Score the screen, show the five rows, and the conversation moves from taste to evidence in about ninety seconds. Keep the honest caveat in your pocket though: a 100 means nothing is measurably broken. It does not mean the work has an idea in it, and that is the half of the job the lab cannot do for you.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Run the full ladder until you can predict which row moves before you click. Then press Add colour on a 100 and write one sentence explaining the loss to a non-designer.
  • Take one screen you shipped recently and score it by hand on the five checks. Be specific: worst contrast ratio, number of distinct font sizes, number of hue families, how many elements are shouting.
  • Rewrite one adjective brief you have actually sent into a constraint brief - one primary action, one type scale, one spacing unit, one accent, and a contrast floor.
  • Generate the same screen twice, once from each brief, in whatever tool you use. Keep both. b2 opens with your pair.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the five-step loop, which steps is AI genuinely strongest at?

Generation is the cheap part and the model is excellent at it. Brief, critique and ship are where your judgement, standard and accountability live - which is exactly why they became more valuable when generation got cheap.

2 · WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 requires 4.5:1 for normal text. When does the 3:1 threshold apply instead?

Size and weight, not role, decide the threshold. A 16px bold heading is still normal text and still owes you 4.5:1 - which is why the lab checks computed size and weight rather than the tag name.

3 · A finished screen scores 100. You turn on "Add colour" and it drops to 81. What does that teach?

The anti-lever adds a fourth hue family, two off-grid paddings and a low-contrast yellow-on-yellow highlight. Colour is not the enemy - undecided colour is. One accent that means something beats three that mean nothing.
